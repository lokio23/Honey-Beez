# Session 5: Meet the Family

**Honey Beez Curriculum | Ages 9-12 | 75 minutes**

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this session, participants will be able to:

- Name at least 5 bee species beyond honeybees and describe one unique trait of each
- Explain the difference between social and solitary bee lifestyles
- Construct a functional bee hotel designed to attract native solitary bees
- Recognize at least 3 bee species by sight using key identification features

---

## Materials Needed

### Supplies
- Bamboo tubes (6-8 inches long, various diameters from 3-10mm) -- 10-15 per participant
- Untreated wood blocks (4x4x6 inches) with pre-drilled holes OR clean tin cans
- Cardboard tubes (paper towel rolls cut to size), hollow plant stems
- Sandpaper (medium grit)
- Non-toxic wood glue
- Twine or wire for hanging
- Craft paper, markers, colored pencils for decorating/labeling

### Printed Materials
- Trading Cards Pack 2 (one set per participant -- species: bumblebee, mason bee, carpenter bee, sweat bee, leafcutter bee, orchid bee, cuckoo bee)
- Field Guide pages (bee family identification sheets)
- Species ID Challenge cards (laminated photo cards of 15 bee species)

### Technology
- Tablet or laptop with Bee Species Explorer module loaded
- Projector or large screen for group viewing
- Speaker for audio (buzz pollination sound clips)

### Reference
- Large printed photos or posters of each bee species discussed
- Globe or world map for showing species ranges
- Magnifying glasses (one per participant)

---

## Narrative Through-Line

> Scout Sunny returns from the longest solo flight of her career. She's been on a reconnaissance mission far beyond the hive's usual territory, and what she found changes everything. Other bees -- thousands of different kinds -- living in ways the Honey Beez Hive never imagined. Bees that live alone. Bees that are metallic green. Bees that vibrate flowers open. Queen Bea calls an emergency assembly: "We are not the only bees that matter. Our survival is tangled with theirs." Guard Gary is skeptical at first -- "If they don't live in hives, are they even real bees?" -- but by the session's end, he's the most passionate advocate for their wild cousins. Nurse Nina realizes that the health of the hive depends on the health of the entire bee family.

## Facilitator Script

### HOOK -- "The Bee Family Reunion" (10 minutes)

**[0:00-0:02] Opening Dramatic Moment**

Start the session with the lights dimmed. Put a single image of a honeybee on screen.

> **Say:** "Raise your hand if you can tell me what this is."

Wait for answers. They'll all say honeybee.

> **Say:** "Great. Now raise your hand if you think you know what a bee looks like."

Everyone raises their hand.

> **Say:** "I'm about to blow your minds. You know ONE bee. There are over 20,000 species of bees on this planet. And here's the wildest part --"

**[0:02-0:05] The Big Reveal**

Click through a rapid slideshow of diverse bee species -- metallic green sweat bees, fuzzy orange bumblebees, enormous carpenter bees, tiny masked bees, iridescent orchid bees.

> **Say:** "NINETY PERCENT of all bee species live completely alone. No hive. No queen. No waggle dance. Just one mom bee, doing everything by herself. The honeybees we've been studying? They're the weird ones. They're the tiny minority that decided to live together."

Let that sink in. Watch their faces.

**[0:05-0:08] Scout Sunny's Report**

> **Say:** "Back at the Honey Beez Hive, Scout Sunny just got back from the longest flight of her life. She went far beyond the usual flower patches, and she found something incredible -- cousins. Thousands of different kinds of cousins, all living totally different lives. Queen Bea called an emergency meeting. Guard Gary said, 'Wait, if they don't have a hive, are they even bees?' What do you think? Are they?"

Take 2-3 responses. Guide toward: yes, they're all bees -- the family is way bigger than we thought.

**[0:08-0:10] Cultural Connection: Ah Muzen Cab**

> **Say:** "Humans have known about this huge bee family for thousands of years. The ancient Maya people in Central America kept stingless bees -- a completely different species from honeybees. They had a god of bees named Ah Muzen Cab. These stingless bees made a special honey that the Maya used as medicine. Beekeeping with stingless bees -- called meliponiculture -- has been going on for over 3,000 years, and some Maya communities still practice it today. So the relationship between humans and the bee family goes way, way back."

Show image of Ah Muzen Cab from the Madrid Codex if available.

---

### EXPLORE -- "Meet the Cousins" (20 minutes)

**[0:10-0:12] Setting Up the Exploration**

> **Say:** "We're going to meet seven of the most amazing members of the bee family. For each one, I want you to listen for their superpower -- the one thing that makes them unlike any other bee. You're going to need this for the challenge later."

Hand out the Field Guide pages. Participants will fill in notes as you go.

**[0:12-0:15] Bumblebees -- The Thunder Bees**

> **Say:** "First up: bumblebees. You've probably seen these fuzzy giants. But here's what most people don't know -- bumblebees can do something called BUZZ POLLINATION."

Play audio clip of buzz pollination if available.

> **Say:** "Some flowers, like tomatoes and blueberries, lock their pollen inside tiny capsules. Honeybees can't get it out. But bumblebees grab onto the flower and vibrate their flight muscles at a specific frequency -- like a tuning fork -- and the pollen explodes out. Without bumblebees, we'd have way fewer tomatoes, peppers, and blueberries."

> **Say:** "And here's the extreme sports fact: bumblebees have been found foraging on Mount Everest at over 18,000 feet. They can fly higher than any other insect. Their big fuzzy bodies trap heat, so they can survive in cold that would kill a honeybee."

*Participants note bumblebee superpower on Field Guide page.*

**[0:15-0:17] Mason Bees -- The Super Pollinators**

> **Say:** "Next: mason bees. These little bees are about the size of a housefly, and they might be the most important pollinator you've never heard of. Here's the math that will make your jaw drop: just 250 mason bees can pollinate an entire acre of apple trees. To do the same job, you'd need roughly 30,000 honeybees. TWO HUNDRED AND FIFTY versus THIRTY THOUSAND."

Write the numbers on a whiteboard. Let kids react.

> **Say:** "Why? Because mason bees are sloppy. Honeybees are neat -- they carefully pack pollen into baskets on their legs. Mason bees belly-flop into flowers and get pollen everywhere. That sloppiness means way more pollen gets transferred between flowers. They're called mason bees because they use mud to build walls inside their nesting tubes -- like tiny bricklayers."

**[0:17-0:19] Carpenter Bees -- The Woodworkers**

> **Say:** "Carpenter bees are the ones that look like bumblebees but have a shiny, hairless abdomen. They're famous -- or infamous -- for drilling perfectly round holes into wood to make their nests. People sometimes think they're destructive, but a single carpenter bee tunnel is actually a tiny engineering marvel. The mom bee drills a tunnel, then creates individual rooms separated by walls of chewed wood pulp, leaving a ball of pollen and one egg in each room."

> **Say:** "Fun fact: male carpenter bees are completely harmless -- they can't sting at all. They just hover around looking tough. Guard Gary would appreciate that move."

**[0:19-0:21] Sweat Bees -- The Jewels**

Show a large photo of a metallic green sweat bee.

> **Say:** "These are sweat bees. Yes, they're real. Yes, they're that color. Some species look like they're made of emeralds or sapphires. They're called sweat bees because some species are attracted to human sweat -- they land on your arm on a hot day to drink the salt. They're totally gentle and rarely sting. There are over 1,000 species of sweat bees in North America alone, and most people have never noticed them because they're so small."

**[0:21-0:23] Leafcutter Bees -- The Tailors**

> **Say:** "Leafcutter bees are the tailors of the bee world. They cut perfectly circular pieces from leaves -- you might have seen leaves with little round holes and wondered what happened. The mama bee uses those leaf circles to wallpaper the insides of her nesting tubes, creating waterproof, cozy chambers for each egg. The precision of these cuts is incredible -- each circle is nearly perfect."

If possible, show a leaf with leafcutter damage and pass it around.

**[0:23-0:26] Orchid Bees -- The Perfume Collectors**

> **Say:** "Orchid bees live in Central and South America, and they are some of the most beautiful insects on Earth -- metallic green, blue, gold, and purple. But their strangest behavior? The males collect scents. They visit orchids not for nectar or pollen, but to scrape up fragrant oils, which they store in special pouches on their back legs. They mix their own custom perfume blend and use it to attract females. They're basically the cologne counters of the rainforest."

**[0:26-0:28] Cuckoo Bees -- The Tricksters**

> **Say:** "Last but definitely not least: cuckoo bees. These are the spies and impostors of the bee world. Named after cuckoo birds, they don't build their own nests or collect their own pollen. Instead, they sneak into other bees' nests and lay their eggs there. The cuckoo bee larva hatches first, eats the food supply, and sometimes even destroys the host bee's egg. It sounds villainous, but cuckoo bees play an important role in keeping other bee populations in balance. Even Nurse Nina has to admit -- nature is complicated."

**[0:28-0:30] Quick Review**

> **Say:** "So let's count. We now know about honeybees from our first sessions, plus bumblebees, mason bees, carpenter bees, sweat bees, leafcutter bees, orchid bees, and cuckoo bees. That's eight. And there are still over 19,992 more species out there. The bee family is enormous, and every single one of them matters."

### MAKE -- "Build a Bee Hotel" (25 minutes)

**[0:30-0:32] Introduction**

> **Say:** "Now that we know about our solitary bee cousins, let's do something real for them. We're going to build bee hotels. These are nesting structures designed for solitary bees like mason bees and leafcutter bees -- bees that naturally nest in hollow tubes and tunnels. A single bee hotel in your yard can become home to dozens of native bees."

**[0:32-0:35] Design Principles**

Walk through the key design rules (write on board):

1. **Tube diameter matters:** Different species need different sizes (3-5mm for small mason bees, 6-8mm for larger species, 8-10mm for leafcutters)
2. **Depth matters:** Tubes should be at least 6 inches deep. Shallow tubes produce only male bees (females lay female eggs at the back, males at the front)
3. **One end must be closed:** Bees need a back wall
4. **Smooth interiors:** Rough edges can tear wings. Sand the openings
5. **No plastic or glass:** Bees need breathable materials (moisture causes mold)
6. **Replaceable tubes:** After a season, old tubes should be swapped out to prevent disease

> **Say:** "Real talk -- a badly made bee hotel can actually hurt bees by spreading disease. So we're going to do this RIGHT."

**[0:35-0:50] Construction Time**

**Option A: Tin Can Hotel (simpler)**
1. Take a clean tin can
2. Cut bamboo tubes and cardboard tubes to fit the length of the can
3. Sand the front openings of each tube smooth
4. Bundle tubes tightly inside the can -- no gaps (bees don't like drafts)
5. Secure with a line of glue at the back
6. Attach twine or wire for hanging

**Option B: Wood Block Hotel (more advanced)**
1. Take the pre-drilled wood block
2. Sand the entrance of each hole smooth
3. Add bamboo tubes to any oversized holes
4. Attach a small roof overhang (piece of bark or extra wood) with glue
5. Attach hanging wire

**Facilitator circulates, helping with:**
- Ensuring tubes are deep enough
- Checking that openings are smooth (demonstrate rubbing a cotton swab through -- it shouldn't snag)
- Making sure tubes are packed tightly with no large gaps
- Answering questions about placement (south-facing, 3-6 feet high, near flowers)

**[0:50-0:55] Finishing Touches**

> **Say:** "Now add a label to your hotel. Include your name, the date, and what types of bees you hope will move in. When you hang this at home, you're going to check it every week. If you see tubes getting capped with mud -- that's a mason bee. Leaf pieces? Leafcutter bee. You just became a landlord for wild bees."

Participants can decorate the exterior (not the tube openings) with nature-themed designs.

### PLAY -- "Species Identification Challenge" (15 minutes)

**[0:55-0:57] Setup**

Spread the laminated photo cards (15 different bee species, including some that are NOT bees -- like hoverflies and bee flies) around the room, taped to walls or placed on tables.

Hand each participant a score sheet.

> **Say:** "Alright, Bee Detectives. Around the room are 15 photos. Some are bees. Some are imposters -- insects that LOOK like bees but aren't. Your job: identify each one. Write down whether it's a bee or not, and if it IS a bee, which type. Use your field guide and trading cards. You have 8 minutes. Go!"

**[0:57-1:05] The Challenge**

Play upbeat music. Let participants move around the room, consulting their field guides, comparing photos to trading cards, and writing answers.

**Identification Tips to Post on the Board:**
- Bees have branched (feathery) body hair; flies have simple hair
- Bees have 4 wings; flies have 2
- Bees have bent antennae; flies have short, stubby antennae
- Bees have narrow waists; wasps have VERY narrow waists

**[1:05-1:10] Reveal and Scoring**

Go through each photo as a group. For each one:
- Show the answer
- Point out the key identification feature
- Award points: 1 point for bee/not-bee correct, bonus point for naming the species

> **Say:** "The tricky ones were the hoverflies -- they evolved to look like bees so predators leave them alone. That's called Batesian mimicry, and it's one of nature's best tricks."

Celebrate the top scorers. Distribute Trading Cards Pack 2 to everyone.

### CONNECT -- "Bee Species Explorer" (10 minutes)

**[1:10-1:13] Digital Module**

Open the Bee Species Explorer on the shared screen (or individual devices if available).

> **Say:** "This digital explorer lets you dive deeper into any species we talked about today -- and dozens more. You can spin 3D models, listen to their buzz frequencies, and see where they live on a world map."

Demo one species together as a group -- click through the bumblebee entry, showing the range map, the buzz pollination audio, and the habitat photos.

**[1:13-1:17] "Which Bee Are You?" Quiz**

> **Say:** "Now for the fun part. There's a quiz in here called 'Which Bee Are You?' It asks about your personality, your habits, and how you'd survive as a bee. Let's all take it."

Give participants 4 minutes to take the quiz individually or in pairs. As results come in, have kids share:
- "I got orchid bee!"
- "I'm a mason bee!"

> **Say:** "Whatever you got -- remember, every type of bee is essential. The world needs all of them. Guard Gary thought only hive bees mattered, but now he knows better. Scout Sunny says our next mission is the most important yet -- because the whole bee family is in trouble. All 20,000 species. And next session, we're going to find out why."

**[1:17-1:20] Wrap-Up and Bridge**

> **Say:** "Take your bee hotels home and hang them this week. South-facing wall, 3-6 feet high, near a garden or wildflowers if possible. Take a photo and bring it next time. Next session is called 'Bees in Trouble,' and it's going to be intense. But remember -- at the Honey Beez Hive, we don't just learn about problems. We solve them."

Hand out Trading Cards Pack 2 and Field Guide pages if not already distributed.

## Differentiation Tips

### For Participants Who Need More Support
- Pre-label the Field Guide pages with species names so participants only need to add notes, not write everything from scratch
- Pair with a buddy during the Species ID Challenge
- Provide a simplified bee hotel design (pre-cut tubes, pre-assembled can)
- Use a matching game format instead of open identification (match photo to name card)

### For Advanced Participants
- Challenge them to add geographic range and conservation status to their Field Guide notes
- During bee hotel construction, have them calculate tube ratios for optimal species diversity
- In the ID challenge, add a "describe how you know" written component
- Ask them to research one additional species not covered in the session and present a 30-second report

### For Participants With Sensory Sensitivities
- Provide noise-canceling headphones during the music portion of the challenge
- Offer gloves for handling bamboo tubes (splinter risk)
- Allow extra time for transitions between activities

## Take-Home Extension Activities

1. **Bee Hotel Monitoring Journal:** Check your bee hotel weekly. Record date, weather, and any activity (capped tubes, bees entering/exiting). Bring observations to Session 7 for the citizen science module.

2. **Backyard Bee Census:** Spend 15 minutes in your yard or a park. How many different types of bees can you spot? Use your field guide and trading cards to try to identify them. Sketch any you can't identify.

3. **Family Interview:** Ask a parent, grandparent, or elder: "Did you see more bees when you were a kid?" Record their answer. This becomes data for Session 6.

4. **Stingless Bee Research:** Look up Melipona bees and Mayan meliponiculture. Write or draw 3 things you learned. How is stingless beekeeping different from honeybee keeping?

5. **Bee Hotel Placement Experiment:** If you have space, make a second hotel and place it facing a different direction. Compare activity over the coming weeks. Which orientation gets more visitors?
