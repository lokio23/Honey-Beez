# Honey Beez Educator Guide

## Program Overview

**Honey Beez** is an 8-session bee education program designed for kids ages 9-12 in after-school and camp settings. The program takes participants from "curious kid" to "Bee Ambassador" through a blend of hands-on science, creative projects, interactive games, and digital experiences.

### Program At a Glance

| Session | Title | Core Topic | Key Activity |
|---------|-------|-----------|-------------|
| 1 | What IS a Bee? | Anatomy & identity | Life-size bee model |
| 2 | Secret Language | Communication | Waggle Dance Challenge |
| 3 | Flower Connection | Pollination | Seed bomb factory |
| 4 | Bee Builders | Engineering & honey | Beeswax candles + hex challenge |
| 5 | Meet the Family | Species diversity | Bee hotel construction |
| 6 | Bees in Trouble | Conservation | Bee Detective mystery |
| 7 | Bee Scientists | Citizen science | Real field survey |
| 8 | Bee Ambassador | Capstone | Zine creation + graduation |

### What Makes Honey Beez Different

- **Beyond honeybees**: We teach about the 20,000+ species worldwide, not just the one everyone knows
- **Bee cognition**: Kids learn that bees do math, recognize faces, and vote democratically
- **Cultural connections**: Bee mythology from Egyptian, Greek, Maya, Celtic, and Hopi traditions
- **Real science contribution**: Session 7 connects kids to actual citizen science platforms
- **Narrative through-line**: A fictional hive colony with named characters keeps kids emotionally invested

## Session Rhythm

Every session follows the same 5-part structure. Kids will come to anticipate and enjoy this rhythm:

### 1. Hook (10 minutes)
Start with something that makes kids go "WHAT?!" This is a mind-blowing fact, a mystery, a video, or a visual that immediately grabs attention. Never start with "Today we're going to learn about..."

**Tips:**
- Stand up, move to the front, change your energy
- Ask kids to predict or guess before revealing the fact
- Use props or visuals whenever possible
- The hook should make them WANT to learn more

### 2. Explore (20 minutes)
Guided inquiry — not a lecture. Ask questions, let kids discover, use hands-on materials. You're a guide, not a teacher.

**Tips:**
- Ask open-ended questions: "Why do you think...?" "What would happen if...?"
- Let kids be wrong — misconceptions are learning opportunities
- Use the Socratic method: answer questions with questions
- Keep it interactive — if you're talking for more than 3 minutes straight, pause

### 3. Make (25 minutes)
The creative/building portion. Kids make something — a model, a craft, a tool, a piece of art. This is where hands-on learning cements concepts.

**Tips:**
- Demo the project briefly, then let kids work
- Circulate and ask questions while they build
- "Tell me about what you're making" is better than "That looks great"
- It's okay if projects look different — creativity over perfection

### 4. Play (15 minutes)
A game, challenge, or competition that reinforces the session's science. Should feel like play, not a quiz.

**Tips:**
- High energy! Get kids moving when possible
- Teams work better than individual competition at this age
- Keep score loosely — the game IS the learning
- Have a simple backup game ready if the planned one doesn't land

### 5. Connect (10 minutes)
Digital module exploration + bridge to next session. Kids use the website, earn their badge sticker, and get a teaser for what's coming next.

**Tips:**
- This is wind-down time — energy should be calming
- Let kids explore freely on the website
- Award badge stickers with a brief ceremony ("You've earned your ___ badge!")
- End with a cliffhanger: "Next time, we're going to discover something that will blow your mind..."

## The Narrative Through-Line

### The Honey Beez Hive Colony

Throughout all 8 sessions, kids follow a fictional honeybee colony. This gives emotional weight to the science.

**Characters:**
- **Queen Bea** — wise, caring, the heart of the colony
- **Scout Sunny** — adventurous, brave, always exploring
- **Nurse Nina** — nurturing, detail-oriented, loves the babies
- **Guard Gary** — protective, loyal, a little intense

**Story Arc:**
- Sessions 1-4: Meet the colony, learn how they live and work together
- Session 5: The colony encounters other bee species
- Session 6: The colony faces threats (pesticides, habitat loss) — crisis point
- Sessions 7-8: Kids become scientists and ambassadors to help the colony and real bees

**How to Use the Characters:**
- Reference them naturally: "Imagine Scout Sunny going out to find flowers today..."
- Use them to explain concepts: "Nurse Nina has to keep the temperature at exactly 95°F"
- Create mini-dramas: "Guard Gary smelled something wrong — a robber bee!"
- Let kids identify with characters: "Who here is a Scout Sunny type? A Nurse Nina?"

## Differentiation Guide

### For Kids Who Need More Support
- Pair them with a buddy during activities
- Provide pre-cut materials for craft projects
- Use picture references alongside text
- Simplify trivia questions (multiple choice instead of open-ended)
- Give extra time and fewer expectations for writing

### For Kids Who Need More Challenge
- Ask deeper "why" questions during Explore
- Assign leadership roles during group activities
- Provide extension challenges (noted in each session plan)
- Encourage them to teach a concept to younger kids
- Give them research questions to investigate between sessions

### For Mixed Groups
- Use flexible grouping — change group composition each session
- Provide tiered activity options ("Try This" / "Challenge Level" / "Expert Mode")
- Let advanced kids be "assistant facilitators"
- Celebrate different types of intelligence (artistic, verbal, kinesthetic, logical)

## Managing Common Situations

### "I'm bored"
Usually means the kid needs more challenge or more autonomy. Try: giving them a leadership role, an extension challenge, or letting them explore the web module early.

### "I'm scared of bees"
Very common! Normalize it. Explain that bees sting only as a last resort (it kills them). Focus on the fascinating science. Session 5 (solitary bees that rarely sting) often converts the fearful kids.

### Bee stings during outdoor sessions
- Have a first aid kit with antihistamine and ice packs
- Know which kids have bee allergies (check registration forms!)
- Kids with severe allergies should have an EpiPen on site
- Teach: stay calm, don't swat, move slowly away

### Rainy days (Session 7 field survey)
The session plan includes an indoor backup using the web module's virtual observation tools and printed photo sets for species identification practice.

### Kids who miss sessions
- Each session is designed to be somewhat self-contained
- Provide a 1-page "catch-up sheet" summarizing what they missed
- Let them explore the relevant web module to fill in gaps
- Don't withhold badges — if they engage with the catch-up materials, award the badge

## Background Science Reference

### Quick Answers to Common Kid Questions

**"Do all bees make honey?"**
No! Only about 5% of bee species make honey. There are over 20,000 bee species and most are solitary — they live alone and don't make honey at all.

**"Why do bees sting?"**
Only female bees can sting, and they only do it to defend themselves or their colony. For honeybees, stinging is a death sentence — the stinger pulls out and the bee dies. So they really don't want to sting you!

**"How do bees make honey?"**
1. Forager bee collects nectar in her honey stomach
2. She passes it mouth-to-mouth to a house bee
3. House bees chew and process the nectar, adding enzymes
4. They spread it in thin layers across the honeycomb
5. They fan it with their wings to evaporate water (from 80% down to about 18%)
6. They cap it with beeswax — done!

**"Are bees going extinct?"**
Some species are in serious trouble (like the Rusty Patched Bumblebee), but bees as a whole aren't going extinct yet. The bigger concern is declining populations and diversity. The good news: conservation efforts are working in many areas, and kids can genuinely help!

**"How far do bees fly?"**
Honeybees typically forage within 1-3 miles of their hive, but can fly up to 5 miles if needed. They fly about 15 mph and visit 50-100 flowers per trip.

**"Why are hexagons special?"**
Hexagons are the most efficient shape for tiling a flat surface with the least perimeter (least wax needed). Bees figured this out through evolution — mathematicians only proved it in 1999!

## Assessment (Optional)

Honey Beez is designed for informal settings where grades aren't the point. But if you need to track learning:

- **Observation**: Note which kids engage deeply vs. superficially
- **Portfolio**: The field guide, zine, and trading cards serve as a natural portfolio
- **Peer teaching**: Can they explain a concept to someone who wasn't there?
- **Citizen science data**: Quality of observations in Session 7
- **Bee Ambassador pledge**: What specific action do they commit to?
