# Session 7: Bee Scientists

**Honey Beez Curriculum | Ages 9-12 | 85 minutes**

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this session, participants will be able to:

- Describe how citizen science works and explain why individual observations have real scientific value
- Use a structured observation protocol to identify, time, and record bee visits to flowers
- Collect, organize, and analyze field data as part of a group dataset
- Identify at least one pattern or trend in their collective data

---

## Materials Needed

### Supplies
- Clipboards (one per participant)
- Pencils (regular pencils -- ink smears in outdoor conditions)
- Stopwatches or timers (one per pair, phone timers work)
- Magnifying glasses (one per participant)
- Flagging tape or small garden stakes (to mark observation stations)

### Printed Materials
- Field Survey Data Sheets (one per participant, detailed format below)
- Field Guide completion pages (final species pages + cover)
- Scientific Observation Protocol card (laminated, one per participant)
- Data Analysis Worksheet (one per participant)

### Technology
- Tablet or laptop with data submission portal loaded
- Projector for group data analysis
- Camera or phone for documenting observations (facilitator use or supervised participant use)

### Outdoor Requirements
- Pre-scouted outdoor area with flowering plants (garden, park, school grounds, or planted containers)
- If no outdoor access: potted flowering plants brought indoors, with printed large-format photos of bee-plant interactions as backup
- Backup plan for rain: indoor data analysis using pre-collected sample datasets

### Preparation (Do Before Session)
- Scout the outdoor area earlier in the day to confirm bee activity
- Set up 4-6 observation stations (mark with flagging tape) at flowering plants
- Prepare a sample dataset on poster paper for the analysis portion
- Pre-load the data submission portal and test connectivity
- Print and laminate the Scientific Observation Protocol cards

---

## Narrative Through-Line

> Scout Sunny has always been the hive's best observer -- she can spot a patch of clover from half a mile away and remember the route after a single flight. But Queen Bea has a new assignment: train EVERYONE in the hive to observe like scientists. "We need data," Queen Bea says. "Not hunches. Not guesses. Data." Nurse Nina starts designing health tracking forms. Guard Gary begins logging entrance traffic with timestamps. Even the youngest worker bees learn to count, record, and report. The Honey Beez Hive is transforming from a colony into a research station. And when they combine all their individual observations, patterns emerge that no single bee could have seen alone. That's the power of citizen science -- many eyes, one picture.

## Facilitator Script

### HOOK -- "Your Eyes Are Scientific Instruments" (10 minutes)

**[0:00-0:03] Live Demo**

Open a citizen science platform on the projector -- iNaturalist, Bumble Bee Watch, or the Great Sunflower Project.

> **Say:** "I'm going to show you something that might change how you think about science. This is [platform name]. Right now, as we sit here, there are people all over the world uploading photos of bees they spotted in their backyards. Regular people. Not scientists in labs. People with phones and curiosity."

Click through recent observations. Show a few entries: the photo, the location, the species identification.

> **Say:** "This person in Ohio photographed a rusty patched bumblebee last Tuesday. That species is endangered. That one photo, uploaded from a backyard, became a data point in a national database that scientists at universities use to track where endangered bees still survive."

**[0:03-0:06] The Power of Numbers**

> **Say:** "One observation is interesting. A hundred observations start to show a pattern. A million observations change what we know about the planet. iNaturalist alone has over 200 million observations from people in every country on Earth. The Great Sunflower Project has been tracking pollinator visits for over 15 years. Bumble Bee Watch has helped scientists map the range of every bumblebee species in North America."

> **Say:** "And here's the part that matters to you: your observations have actual scientific value. Not pretend value. Not 'oh, that's nice, honey' value. ACTUAL value. When you record a bee on a flower today -- accurately, carefully, using the right protocol -- that data point can end up in a peer-reviewed scientific study. You're not playing scientist. You ARE a scientist."

**[0:06-0:08] How Real Scientists Study Bees**

> **Say:** "Professional bee researchers use the same basic method you're going to learn today. They go to a site. They watch a patch of flowers for a set amount of time. They count how many bees visit, what species they are, what flowers they go to, and what they do there. The fancy lab equipment comes later -- the foundation is careful observation. Scout Sunny has been doing this her whole life. Today she trains you."

**[0:08-0:10] Observation vs. Interpretation**

> **Say:** "Before we go outside, we need to talk about the most important rule in science: the difference between observation and interpretation."

Hold up an apple with a brown spot.

> **Say:** "Observation: 'This apple has a brown spot that is about 1 centimeter wide on the left side.' Interpretation: 'This apple is rotten.' See the difference? The observation describes what you can directly see, measure, or count. The interpretation is what you THINK it means. Both matter, but in your data collection, we record observations first. Interpretations come later, when we look at all the data together."

Quick practice round:
- Show a photo of a bee on a flower. Ask: "Give me an observation." (Good: "A black and yellow insect is sitting on a purple flower.") "Now give me an interpretation." (Good: "The bee is collecting nectar.")
- Reinforce: observations go on the data sheet, interpretations go in the notes column.

---

### EXPLORE -- "The Protocol" (20 minutes)

**[0:10-0:13] Introducing the Field Survey Protocol**

Hand out the Scientific Observation Protocol cards and Field Survey Data Sheets.

> **Say:** "Real scientists follow protocols -- step-by-step procedures that make sure everyone collects data the same way. If I count bees for 5 minutes and you count bees for 20 minutes, our numbers can't be compared. If I'm watching sunflowers and you're watching clover, we're measuring different things. The protocol standardizes everything so our data fits together like puzzle pieces."

Walk through the protocol card:

**Honey Beez Field Survey Protocol:**

| Step | Action | Time |
|------|--------|------|
| 1 | Choose your observation station (one flowering plant or patch) | -- |
| 2 | Record station info: plant type, flower color, approximate number of blooms | 1 min |
| 3 | Start timer: observe for exactly 5 minutes | 5 min |
| 4 | Record every bee visit: time of arrival, bee description, behavior observed | During observation |
| 5 | After 5 minutes, stop. Count your total visits. | 1 min |
| 6 | Repeat for a second 5-minute observation at the same or different station | 5 min |
| 7 | Complete the summary section of your data sheet | 2 min |

**[0:13-0:17] Data Sheet Walkthrough**

Go through each field on the data sheet:

> **Say:** "Let's look at what you'll be recording."

- **Observer name and date** -- "So we know whose data this is"
- **Station number** -- "Match it to the flagging tape marker outside"
- **Plant description** -- "Common name if you know it, otherwise describe: 'Purple spiky flower, 30cm tall, about 20 open blooms'"
- **Weather conditions** -- "Sunny, partly cloudy, overcast, windy? Temperature if you can estimate. Bees are more active in warm, calm weather."
- **Observation table columns:**
  - Time: "When the bee arrived, to the nearest minute"
  - Bee description: "Size (tiny/small/medium/large), color, fuzzy or smooth, any patterns"
  - Bee ID guess: "Your best guess using the field guide -- bumblebee, sweat bee, honeybee, etc. It's okay to write 'unknown'"
  - Flower visited: "Which specific flower the bee went to"
  - Behavior: "What was it doing? Collecting pollen, drinking nectar, resting, flying between flowers?"
  - Duration: "How long did it stay? Count seconds if you can"
- **Notes column** -- "Anything else you noticed. Weird behavior. Other insects. Your interpretations go here."

**[0:17-0:22] Practice Round (Indoor)**

> **Say:** "Before we go out, let's practice. I'm going to show you a 60-second video of bees visiting flowers. Fill in one row of your data sheet as if you're doing a real observation."

Play a short video clip of bees foraging (or describe a scenario verbally if no video is available). Have participants fill in one row. Then review together:

> **Ask:** "What did you write for bee description?" Take 3-4 answers. Model good descriptions: "Medium-sized, fuzzy, black and yellow bands, pollen on back legs" is better than "a bee."

> **Ask:** "What did you put in the behavior column?" Reinforce specific language: "landed on flower, pushed head into center, moved to next flower after 8 seconds" is better than "getting food."

**[0:22-0:25] Field Briefing**

> **Say:** "When we go outside, you'll work in pairs. One person observes and calls out what they see. The other person records. Switch roles after the first 5-minute observation. A few rules for the field:"

Write on board:
1. **Stay quiet near your station.** Loud noises can scare bees away.
2. **Stay still.** Bees won't bother you if you don't bother them. No swatting.
3. **Stay at least 1 foot from the flowers.** Close enough to see, far enough to not interfere.
4. **If you see zero bees in 5 minutes, that IS data.** Record it. Absence of bees is scientifically valuable.
5. **Do NOT touch or catch any bees.**

**[0:25-0:30] Equipment Check and Move Outside**

> **Say:** "Grab your clipboard, data sheet, protocol card, field guide, magnifying glass, and a pencil. Pair up. We're heading out. Scout Sunny says: sharp eyes, steady hands, quiet feet."

Line up and head to the outdoor observation area.

---

### MAKE -- "Field Survey" (25 minutes)

**[0:30-0:32] Station Assignment**

At the outdoor area, assign each pair to a station (marked with flagging tape). If you have more pairs than stations, stagger start times or double up with pairs observing the same station from different angles (this actually produces useful comparative data).

> **Say:** "Find your station. Fill in the station info at the top of your sheet -- plant type, flower color, number of blooms. Then wait for my signal to start."

**[0:32-0:37] Observation Round 1 (5 minutes)**

> **Say:** "Observers ready? Recorders ready? Timers set for 5 minutes? Three... two... one... BEGIN."

**During the observation, circulate quietly among pairs:**
- Help with identification: "That looks like it might be a sweat bee -- see how small it is? Check the size chart on your field guide."
- Encourage detail: "What color is the pollen on its legs?"
- Reassure zero-sighting pairs: "That's okay. Write down what you DO see. Are there other insects? What's the weather doing?"
- Take photos of any bees you can capture on camera for later identification

**[0:37-0:39] Switch and Transition**

> **Say:** "Time! Observers, you're now recorders. Recorders, you're now observers. Same station OR you can move to a different station if you want to compare. Reset your timers. Fill in new station info if you moved."

**[0:39-0:44] Observation Round 2 (5 minutes)**

> **Say:** "Round 2. Three... two... one... BEGIN."

Continue circulating. This round, participants are usually more confident and detailed in their observations.

**[0:44-0:47] Wrap-Up Round and Summary**

> **Say:** "Time! Great work. Now take 3 minutes to fill in the summary section of your data sheet. How many total bee visits did you record across both rounds? How many different types of bees did you see? What was the most common behavior you observed?"

**[0:47-0:50] Quick Share**

Before heading inside, do a rapid round-robin while still outdoors:

> **Say:** "Each pair, give me your one most interesting observation. Just one sentence."

Go around quickly. Common highlights:
- "We saw a tiny metallic green bee that we think is a sweat bee!"
- "The bumblebees only visited the purple flowers, not the white ones."
- "We had zero bees for 3 minutes, then 4 showed up in the last 2 minutes."
- "A bee visited the same flower three times in a row."

> **Say:** "You just heard a dozen observations from one location on one day. Imagine what we'd learn with 100 days of data. That's citizen science. Let's go inside and crunch these numbers."

Head back indoors.

**[0:50-0:55] Data Compilation**

On a large poster paper or projected spreadsheet, create a group data table:

| Station | Plant Type | Total Bee Visits (10 min) | Most Common Bee Type | Most Common Behavior |
|---------|-----------|--------------------------|---------------------|---------------------|
| 1       | Lavender  | 7                        | Bumblebee           | Nectar collecting   |
| 2       | Sunflower | 12                       | Honeybee            | Pollen collecting   |
| 3       | Clover    | 3                        | Unknown small bee   | Nectar collecting   |
| ...     | ...       | ...                      | ...                 | ...                 |

Have each pair call out their data. Record it for everyone to see.

---

### PLAY -- "Data Analysis Challenge" (15 minutes)

**[0:55-0:57] Introduction**

Hand out the Data Analysis Worksheet.

> **Say:** "Scientists don't just collect data -- they look for patterns. Your challenge: using our group data, answer as many of these questions as you can in 10 minutes. Work with your partner."

**[0:57-1:07] Analysis Questions**

The worksheet includes:

1. **Which plant had the most bee visits?** Why do you think that is?
2. **Which plant had the fewest?** What might explain that?
3. **Did any one type of bee prefer a specific flower color or type?** (This is called "floral preference.")
4. **What was the most common behavior across all stations?** What does that tell us about what bees were doing at this time of day?
5. **Were there any stations with zero or very few bees?** Look at those stations -- what's different about them? (Position, sun exposure, flower type, proximity to other plants?)
6. **BONUS: Calculate the average number of bee visits per station.** Add up all visits and divide by the number of stations.
7. **BONUS: If our data represents one day at one location, what would we need to do to draw stronger scientific conclusions?** (Answer: more days, more locations, more seasons, more observers.)
8. **WILD CARD: What's one question our data CANNOT answer, but you wish it could?**

**[1:07-1:10] Group Discussion**

Go through the questions as a group. For each one, take answers from multiple pairs.

Key facilitation points:

- **When discussing flower preferences:** "You just discovered something real. Scientists have published entire papers on which flowers attract which bees. Your data, from today, contributes to that knowledge."
- **When discussing zero-bee stations:** "This is actually critical data. Knowing where bees AREN'T is just as important as knowing where they are. If a park has beautiful flowers but no bees, that tells us something might be wrong in that environment."
- **When discussing sample size:** "This is one of the biggest concepts in science. One day of data is a snapshot. A year of data is a story. Ten years of data is a map of change. That's why citizen science projects need people collecting data over and over, for years."

> **Say:** "Guard Gary always says he knows exactly how many bees enter and leave the hive each day. He's been doing his own citizen science this whole time -- he just didn't know it had a name."

---

### CONNECT -- "Data Submission + Field Guide Completion" (10 minutes)

**[1:10-1:14] Data Submission Portal**

Open the data submission portal on the projector.

> **Say:** "Here's where your data goes from a piece of paper to part of a real database. Let me show you how to enter your observations."

Walk through the submission process:
1. Enter observer name and date
2. Enter location (general area is fine -- no home addresses)
3. Enter each observation with plant type, bee type, visit count, and weather
4. Upload any photos taken during the survey (facilitator phone)
5. Submit

If time and devices allow, have each pair submit their own data. Otherwise, compile and submit as a group with the facilitator entering data while participants watch.

> **Say:** "That's it. Your data is now in the system. It's real. It counts. The next time a researcher studies pollinator activity in this area, your observations are part of the picture."

**[1:14-1:18] Field Guide Completion**

Hand out the final Field Guide pages and cover.

> **Say:** "You've been building your Field Guide since Session 5. Today, you add the final pages -- your own field notes from today's survey. This guide is now a real scientific reference document that YOU created. Write your name on the cover. You earned it."

Participants compile their field guide pages, add today's observation notes, and assemble the final guide.

**[1:18-1:20] Bridge to Final Session**

> **Say:** "Next time is our final session. Session 8: 'I Am a Bee Ambassador.' You've learned the science. You've met the bee family. You've faced the threats. You've collected real data. Now it's time to take everything you know and share it with the world. Nurse Nina says the strongest medicine is knowledge that spreads. You're going to create something to share -- and we're going to celebrate everything you've accomplished."

> **Say:** "Here's your assignment for next session: think about ONE thing you want to tell the world about bees. One fact, one story, one call to action. It can be anything from our eight sessions. Bring that idea with you. You'll need it."

## Differentiation Tips

### For Participants Who Need More Support
- Pre-fill the station info section of the data sheet before the session
- Pair with a confident observer; assign the "recorder" role first (easier entry point)
- Provide a simplified data sheet with fewer columns (just: Time, What I Saw, What It Did)
- During analysis, provide the answers to questions 1-3 and ask them to verify using the data
- Use picture-based bee ID (match to photos) rather than text descriptions

### For Advanced Participants
- Give them a second data sheet and challenge them to create their own custom observation category (e.g., track flight patterns between flowers, measure time-between-visits)
- During analysis, ask them to create a simple bar graph of visits-per-station
- Have them calculate statistical measures: range, mode, and median of visit counts
- Challenge them to write a one-paragraph "abstract" of the group's findings, as if for a scientific paper
- Ask them to identify confounding variables in the survey (wind, time of day, observer experience)

### For Days With No Bee Activity
- This can happen if it's too cool, windy, or overcast. Have a backup plan ready.
- Option A: Use a pre-collected sample dataset. Tell participants: "Real scientists deal with this too. Sometimes you go into the field and see nothing. But we have data from a previous survey to analyze."
- Option B: Focus the observation on ALL pollinators (butterflies, flies, beetles) and compare to what a bee-focused survey would look like
- Option C: Shift to indoor observation of high-quality video footage, using the same data sheet protocol
- Always validate: "A survey with zero bees is still a valid survey. That data point matters."

### For Indoor-Only Settings
- Set up potted flowering plants near windows 24-48 hours before the session
- Use high-resolution video of bee foraging (slowed down) for the observation rounds
- Supplement with printed high-quality photo sets showing different species on different flowers
- The data analysis portion works identically with provided sample data

## Take-Home Extension Activities

1. **Repeat Survey at Home:** Using the same protocol and data sheet, conduct a 10-minute survey in your yard, a park, or a community garden. Compare your results to what we found today. What's similar? What's different?

2. **Join a Real Citizen Science Project:** With a parent or guardian, create an account on one of these platforms and submit at least one observation:
   - iNaturalist (any living thing, including bees)
   - Bumble Bee Watch (specifically for bumblebees)
   - The Great Sunflower Project (pollinator counts on sunflowers and other plants)

3. **Weather-Bee Connection:** Do your home survey on two different days -- one sunny, one cloudy (or one warm, one cool). Compare the data. How does weather affect bee activity?

4. **Field Guide Show-and-Tell:** Show your completed field guide to a family member. Teach them to identify three bee species. Quiz them.

5. **Data Visualization:** Take the group data from today's session and create a visual representation -- a bar chart, a pie chart, or even an illustrated map of our observation stations. Bring it to Session 8 to share.
