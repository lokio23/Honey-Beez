# Session 6: Bees in Trouble

**Honey Beez Curriculum | Ages 9-12 | 80 minutes**

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this session, participants will be able to:

- Identify the "4 Ps" threatening bee populations (Pests, Poor nutrition, Pesticides, Pathogens) plus climate change and habitat loss
- Explain Colony Collapse Disorder and why it alarmed scientists worldwide
- Analyze evidence to determine the cause of a fictional colony collapse (scientific reasoning)
- Describe at least 3 concrete actions that individuals and communities can take to help bees

---

## Materials Needed

### Supplies
- "Bee Detective" mystery kits (one per team of 3-4, contents detailed below)
- Large poster paper or butcher paper (one sheet per team)
- Markers, colored pencils
- Index cards (20 per team for Nectar Economics)
- Dice (2 per team)
- Printed "Nectar Economics" game boards and rule sheets

### Bee Detective Kit Contents (per team, in a sealed envelope)
- Evidence Card 1: Colony inspection report (declining population numbers over 6 months)
- Evidence Card 2: Lab analysis (pesticide residue detected on pollen samples)
- Evidence Card 3: Weather data (drought conditions, record heat, reduced wildflower bloom)
- Evidence Card 4: Mite inspection report (Varroa mite levels elevated)
- Evidence Card 5: Neighboring farm report (new monoculture operation, heavy spraying)
- Evidence Card 6: Beekeeper's journal entries (noting unusual bee behavior)
- Red herring card: Report of a nearby cell phone tower (a debunked theory)
- Magnifying glass
- "Case Closed" report form

### Printed Materials
- Infographic #3: "The State of the Bees" (one per participant)
- "4 Ps" reference cards
- "What You Can Do" action cards

### Technology
- Projector or large screen
- Tablet or laptop with Citizen Science Dashboard preview loaded

## Narrative Through-Line

> The Honey Beez Hive is thriving -- until it isn't. Scout Sunny returns from a flight to report something terrifying: a neighboring colony has gone silent. Thousands of bees, gone. No bodies. No explanation. Just empty combs and a confused, dwindling cluster around Queen Bea's counterpart in the other hive. Queen Bea convenes an urgent council. "This could happen to us," she says. Nurse Nina begins investigating the health records. Guard Gary doubles the perimeter watch. But the real threat isn't something Gary can fight at the door. It's invisible. It's systemic. And understanding it is the first step toward surviving it. By session's end, the hive has a plan -- not just to protect themselves, but to sound the alarm for bees everywhere.

## Facilitator Script

### HOOK -- "The Vanishing" (10 minutes)

**[0:00-0:03] The Empty Hive**

Dim the lights. Display an image of a healthy, bustling hive. Then slowly crossfade to an image of an empty hive -- abandoned combs, no bees.

> **Say:** "This hive had 60,000 bees in it. A thriving colony. The beekeeper checked on them in October -- everything was fine. She came back in November, and they were gone. Not dead on the ground. Not killed by a bear. Just... gone. Vanished. The honey was still there. The baby bees were still in their cells. But every adult bee had disappeared."

Pause. Let the room sit with that.

> **Say:** "In the winter of 2023-2024, beekeepers across the United States lost almost half their colonies -- roughly 1.7 million hives. And this wasn't a one-time event. It's been happening year after year, getting worse."

**[0:03-0:06] Colony Collapse Disorder**

> **Say:** "Scientists gave this mystery a name: Colony Collapse Disorder, or CCD. It first made global news around 2006, when beekeepers started reporting that their bees were vanishing overnight. No one knew why. The media went wild. 'Are cell phones killing bees?' 'Is it aliens?' No. It wasn't aliens. But the real answer turned out to be even scarier, because it wasn't just one thing. It was everything, all at once."

**[0:06-0:08] Framing With Hope**

This is critical. Pause and shift your tone to something warm and firm.

> **Say:** "Before we go further, I need to tell you something important. What we're about to learn is heavy. Bees are in real trouble. But here's what I want you to hold onto: we are not too late. Scientists, beekeepers, farmers, kids -- people are fighting for bees every single day. And by the end of today, you'll have the tools to join that fight. At the Honey Beez Hive, Nurse Nina says the same thing: 'Knowing the problem is the first step to being the cure.' So let's know the problem."

**[0:08-0:10] Cultural Connection: Hopi Bee Teachings**

> **Say:** "The Hopi people of the American Southwest have long seen bees as messengers between the physical world and the spirit world. In Hopi tradition, bees represent community, hard work, and the balance of nature. When bees struggle, it's a sign that the whole web of life is out of balance. The Hopi teach that humans have a responsibility to maintain that balance. Today, as we learn about the threats bees face, think about what balance means -- and what happens when it tips."

---

### EXPLORE -- "The 4 Ps + 2" (20 minutes)

**[0:10-0:12] Setting Up the Framework**

Draw a large hexagon on the whiteboard. Label it "HEALTHY COLONY" in the center. Draw six arrows pointing inward, labeled with the six threats. (Or reveal a pre-made poster.)

> **Say:** "Scientists have identified six major forces pushing against bees. Four of them start with P, so researchers call them the '4 Ps.' Then there are two more huge ones. Let's go through each."

Hand out the "4 Ps" reference cards.

**[0:12-0:14] Pest #1: Varroa Mites**

> **Say:** "Threat number one: PESTS. And the number-one pest is the Varroa destructor mite. Yes, that's its actual scientific name. Destructor. This mite is the size of a pinhead, and it latches onto a bee like a tick, sucking its blood -- technically called hemolymph. One mite doesn't kill a bee, but it weakens it AND spreads viruses at the same time. Imagine getting the flu from a tick bite while it's also draining your energy. In Nurse Nina's medical bay, Varroa is enemy number one."

> **Ask:** "If one mite can spread a virus to one bee, and that bee goes back to a hive of 60,000 bees... what happens?" Take responses.

**[0:14-0:16] Poor Nutrition**

> **Say:** "Threat two: POOR NUTRITION. Bees need a diverse diet -- pollen from many different flowers gives them all the proteins, fats, and nutrients they need. But when thousands of acres are planted with just one crop -- that's called monoculture -- bees get the equivalent of eating nothing but plain rice. Every single meal. They survive, but they get weak. Their immune systems crash. And after that one crop finishes blooming? There's nothing left. It's like a food desert for bees."

> **Ask:** "Think about your own diet. What would happen if you ate only one food for months?" Take 2-3 responses.

**[0:16-0:18] Pesticides**

> **Say:** "Threat three: PESTICIDES. Farmers use chemicals to kill insects that damage their crops. The problem? Those chemicals often can't tell the difference between a pest and a pollinator. One class of pesticides called neonicotinoids is especially dangerous for bees. These chemicals are absorbed into the entire plant -- the stems, the leaves, the pollen, and the nectar. A bee visiting a treated flower drinks pesticide with every sip. It doesn't kill them instantly. Instead, it scrambles their navigation system. Scout Sunny wouldn't be able to find her way home. She'd fly in circles until she died."

This is often the fact that hits kids hardest. Give a moment.

**[0:18-0:20] Pathogens**

> **Say:** "Threat four: PATHOGENS -- diseases caused by viruses, bacteria, and fungi. Bees get sick just like we do. Deformed Wing Virus cripples their wings so they can't fly. Nosema is a fungal gut infection that weakens entire colonies. And remember the Varroa mites? They're like dirty needles, spreading these diseases from bee to bee."

**[0:20-0:23] Climate Change**

> **Say:** "Beyond the 4 Ps, there's climate change. Warming temperatures are throwing off the timing between bees and flowers. A wildflower might bloom two weeks early because of a warm spring, but the bees that pollinate it emerge on their old schedule. They show up and the flowers are already gone. Scientists call this 'phenological mismatch' -- a fancy way of saying nature's clock is broken. Extreme weather events -- droughts, floods, late freezes -- also destroy the flowers bees depend on."

**[0:23-0:26] Habitat Loss**

> **Say:** "And finally: habitat loss. Every time a meadow becomes a parking lot, every time a hedgerow is torn out for a bigger field, every time a lawn replaces a wildflower patch -- bees lose their homes and their food. In the last 50 years, we've lost huge amounts of wild bee habitat. For solitary bees who nest in the ground, even compacting soil with heavy machinery can destroy their homes."

**[0:26-0:30] The Combination Effect**

> **Say:** "Here's the thing that makes this so complicated: it's almost never just ONE of these threats. A bee weakened by poor nutrition is more vulnerable to pesticides. A mite-infested colony is less able to handle a late freeze. The threats multiply each other. Scientists call this 'synergistic stress.' Guard Gary can fight off one intruder at the hive entrance. But what if there are six intruders, all at once, coming from different directions? That's what bees are dealing with."

> **Ask the group:** "If you were Queen Bea, and your colony was facing all six of these threats, which one would you tackle first? Why?" Take 3-4 responses. There's no right answer -- the point is that it's overwhelming, which is why bees need help from humans.

### MAKE -- "Bee Detective Mystery" (25 minutes)

**[0:30-0:33] Setting the Scene**

> **Say:** "A beekeeper named Maria has called the Honey Beez Emergency Hotline. Her colony is collapsing. She needs Bee Detectives -- that's you -- to figure out what happened and recommend a solution. Each team will receive a sealed evidence packet. Your job: examine ALL the evidence, determine the primary cause of the collapse, and present your findings."

Divide into teams of 3-4. Hand each team a sealed "Bee Detective" evidence kit.

**[0:33-0:35] Rules of Investigation**

> **Say:** "Real scientists don't jump to conclusions. Here are your detective rules:"

Write on board:
1. Read ALL evidence before making a theory
2. Look for connections between pieces of evidence
3. One piece of evidence can be a RED HERRING -- misleading
4. Your final report must include: the primary cause, supporting evidence (at least 2 pieces), and your recommended action for Maria

**[0:35-0:50] Investigation Time**

Teams open their evidence packets and begin analyzing. Circulate among teams.

**Facilitator guidance during investigation:**

- If a team fixates on the cell tower card immediately: "Remember, real scientists look at ALL the evidence before concluding. Is there stronger evidence pointing somewhere else?"
- If a team is stuck: "Try laying all the evidence cards out on the table. Look for connections. Which pieces of evidence support each other?"
- If a team finishes early: "Can you identify a SECONDARY cause? In real colony collapses, there's usually more than one factor."

**The intended "solution":** The colony is suffering from a combination of pesticide exposure (from the neighboring farm's spraying) and nutritional stress (drought reducing wildflower availability), compounded by elevated Varroa mite levels. The cell tower card is the red herring. Strong teams will identify the interaction between all three real factors.

**[0:50-0:55] Case Presentations**

Each team presents their findings in 2 minutes:
- What they think happened
- Which evidence supports their theory
- What Maria should do

> **After all presentations, say:** "Notice how most teams identified more than one cause? That's EXACTLY how real bee science works. Colony collapses are almost never simple. The best thing Maria can do is address multiple factors: talk to the neighboring farm about pesticide timing, treat for Varroa mites, and plant diverse wildflowers to supplement nutrition during drought."

> **Say:** "And the cell tower? That theory went viral online years ago, but scientists tested it thoroughly and found no connection. Lesson: in science, popular doesn't mean proven. Always follow the evidence."

---

### PLAY -- "Nectar Economics" Card Game (15 minutes)

**[0:55-0:57] Introduction**

> **Say:** "Now let's experience the life of a foraging bee in a world full of threats. This is Nectar Economics -- a card game based on a real scientific concept called Optimal Foraging Theory. The question: How does a bee decide which flowers to visit when energy is limited and dangers are everywhere?"

**[0:57-0:58] Rules Explanation**

Each team of 2-3 is a foraging squad. Deal each team:
- 10 Flower Cards (each showing nectar reward: 1-5 points, and distance: 1-3 energy cost)
- 5 Threat Cards (shuffled into the flower deck: Pesticide Spray = lose all points from this round, Drought = all flowers worth 1 less point, Mite Infection = every round costs 1 extra energy, Habitat Loss = remove 2 flower cards from play, Late Freeze = skip one turn)
- Starting energy: 20 points

**How to play:**
1. Each round, flip 3 cards from your deck
2. Choose which flowers to visit (spend energy equal to distance cost, gain nectar points)
3. If you flip a Threat Card, its effect applies immediately
4. Goal: collect the most nectar before running out of energy
5. Play 5 rounds

**[0:58-1:08] Game Play**

Teams play through 5 rounds. The game naturally demonstrates:
- Resource scarcity (energy runs out fast)
- Threat compounding (multiple threats in one round can be devastating)
- Strategic decision-making (sometimes skipping a low-reward flower saves energy for a better one)
- How habitat loss literally removes options

**[1:08-1:10] Debrief**

> **Say:** "What happened to teams that got hit by multiple threats in one round? How did you change your strategy when flower options disappeared? This is what foraging bees experience every day. The bees that survive are the ones that adapt -- but there's a limit to how much any creature can adapt when the threats keep piling up."

> **Ask:** "What would have helped your team survive longer?" Guide toward: more flowers (habitat), fewer threats (reduced pesticides), better nutrition (diverse flowers).

### CONNECT -- "Citizen Science Dashboard + The Road Forward" (10 minutes)

**[1:10-1:13] Citizen Science Preview**

> **Say:** "Next session, we're going to become real scientists. You're going to collect actual data that contributes to real research. Let me show you what that looks like."

Open the Citizen Science Dashboard on the projector. Show:
- A map of bee observation data points
- How individual observations add up to reveal population trends
- What happens when kids and families contribute data

> **Say:** "See those dots? Each one is a person -- some of them your age -- who spotted a bee, recorded what they saw, and uploaded it. When you add hundreds of thousands of those observations together, scientists can track which species are thriving and which are disappearing. Your eyes and your phone camera become scientific instruments."

**[1:13-1:17] The Hope Section**

This is the most important part of the session. End on strength, not fear.

> **Say:** "I promised you at the beginning that we wouldn't just learn about problems. So here's the truth: there is SO much being done."

Tick through examples rapidly:
- "Cities are banning the worst pesticides on public land"
- "Farmers are planting wildflower strips along their fields -- called pollinator hedgerows"
- "Scientists bred Varroa-resistant honeybees that bite mites off each other"
- "The Bee Informed Partnership tracks colony health across the entire country"
- "Kids like you are building bee hotels, planting gardens, and collecting data"

> **Say:** "Queen Bea told the hive: 'We cannot fight what we do not understand.' Now you understand. And next session, you become the scientists. Nurse Nina is already preparing the field kits."

**[1:17-1:20] Wrap-Up**

Hand out Infographic #3 and "What You Can Do" action cards.

> **Say:** "Take the infographic home and put it somewhere your family will see it -- the fridge, a bulletin board. Start a conversation. And look at your action cards: these are things you can do THIS WEEK. Pick one. Just one. And do it before next session."

## Differentiation Tips

### For Participants Who Need More Support
- Pre-highlight key words on the evidence cards
- Assign specific roles within the detective team (Evidence Reader, Note Taker, Theory Builder) so everyone has a clear job
- Simplify the Nectar Economics game by removing 2 Threat Cards
- Provide sentence starters for the case presentation: "We believe the colony collapsed because..."

### For Advanced Participants
- Add an extra evidence card to their detective kit that introduces ambiguity (a second possible primary cause)
- In Nectar Economics, add a "cooperative" variant where teams can trade flower cards -- demonstrating how biodiversity helps everyone
- Ask them to calculate the economic impact: "If each hive pollinates $200 worth of crops, and 1.7 million hives were lost, how much crop pollination was lost?" (Answer: $340 million -- and the real number is even higher)
- Challenge them to write a one-paragraph letter to a local official about pollinator protection

### For Emotionally Sensitive Participants
- The topic of colony collapse can be upsetting, especially for kids who love animals. Watch for signs of distress
- Emphasize the hope framing repeatedly: "This is solvable. People are working on it. You are part of the solution."
- Allow participants to step out briefly if needed
- Pair sensitive participants with empathetic peers during the detective activity

## Take-Home Extension Activities

1. **Family Threat Audit:** Walk around your home and yard with a family member. Can you spot any of the "4 Ps + 2" in your immediate environment? (Pesticide containers in the garage? A large monoculture lawn? Lack of wildflowers?) Write down what you find and one change your family could make.

2. **News Watch:** Find one current news article about bees or pollinators. Summarize it in 3 sentences. Does it relate to any of the six threats we discussed? Bring it to the next session.

3. **Action Card Challenge:** Pick one action from your "What You Can Do" card and actually do it this week. Take a photo or write about the experience. Options include: planting a pollinator-friendly plant, talking to a neighbor about reducing pesticide use, or writing a letter to a local park about creating pollinator habitat.

4. **Interview a Farmer or Gardener:** If you know someone who grows food, ask them: "Do you see bees on your plants? Have you noticed any changes over the years?" Record their answers.

5. **Nectar Economics Home Edition:** Teach the Nectar Economics card game to a family member or friend. After playing, explain what the threats represent in real life.
