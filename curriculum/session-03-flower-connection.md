# Session 3: "The Flower Connection"

**Honey Beez Curriculum | Ages 9-12 | 80 minutes**

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this session, participants will be able to:

- Explain the mechanics of pollination and why it is essential to the global food supply
- Describe at least 3 ways flowers attract and guide pollinators (color, scent, nectar guides, UV patterns)
- Define buzz pollination and explain why some plants require it
- Create seed bombs using appropriate native plant species and explain how they support pollinators

---

## Materials Needed

### Supplies
- Air-dry clay or modeling clay (for seed bombs — approximately 1 cup per participant)
- Compost or potting soil (for seed bombs — approximately 1/2 cup per participant)
- Native wildflower seed mix (research your region — avoid invasive species; approximately 1 tablespoon per participant)
- Small bowls for mixing (1 per pair)
- Wax paper or parchment paper (for drying seed bombs)
- Pipe cleaners (assorted colors — for flower models during relay)
- Pom-poms or cotton balls (to represent pollen)
- Cheetos or cheese puff snacks (to represent pollen during relay — sticky, powdery, fun)
- Paper towels and wet wipes
- Plastic cups (for relay race stations)
- Chenille stems and tissue paper (for flower making)
- Cardboard tubes (toilet paper or paper towel rolls — for buzz pollination demo)

### Printed Materials
- Lifecycle Poster (1 per participant — the print for this session)
- UV flower comparison photos (printed in color — regular light vs. UV light side by side)
- Pollination vocabulary cards (1 set per table)
- "What Would Disappear?" food cards (images of foods that depend on pollinators)
- Seed bomb instruction cards (1 per pair)

### Technology
- Laptop/tablet with projector or large screen
- UV flower photography slideshow queued (search for "flowers in ultraviolet light" — many stunning examples from research labs)
- Audio clip of a bee buzzing at middle C (261.6 Hz) and a tuning fork if available
- Pollination Puzzle Game loaded and ready
- Speaker for audio

### Facilitator Prep
- Mix clay and soil for seed bombs in advance (or let participants mix as part of the activity)
- Set up 5 "flower stations" around the room for the pollination relay (each station is a plastic cup with pom-poms inside, representing a flower with pollen)
- Print and cut the "What Would Disappear?" food cards
- Test audio equipment with the buzz pollination sound clip
- Lay out wax paper drying stations for seed bombs

---

## Narrative Through-Line

> Nurse Nina has taken over today's briefing. She handles everything inside the hive — feeding larvae, processing nectar, maintaining comb — but she also understands the most critical relationship in the natural world: the partnership between bees and flowers. Today, she's going to show us what flowers look like through a bee's eyes (spoiler: it's stunning), explain why this partnership keeps the planet fed, and teach us how to fight back against habitat loss — one seed bomb at a time.

## Facilitator Script

### HOOK — "The Hidden World" (10 minutes)

**[0:00 - 0:04] The Reveal**

*Display a photo of a plain-looking yellow flower — something like a black-eyed Susan or sunflower in normal light.*

> "Raise your hand if this flower looks interesting to you."

*Likely a few hands, polite but not enthusiastic.*

> "Fair enough. It's a nice flower. Yellow, some petals, pretty standard. But here's the question: what if I told you that you are PHYSICALLY INCAPABLE of seeing what this flower actually looks like?"

*Click to reveal the same flower photographed under ultraviolet light. The UV image should show dramatic patterns — dark center targets, glowing nectar guides, contrasting lines pointing toward the center.*

*Pause for reactions.*

> "THIS is what a bee sees. Those lines you see pointing toward the center of the flower? Those are called NECTAR GUIDES. They are invisible to human eyes. To a bee, every flower is covered in runway lights, like an airport landing strip, screaming 'LAND HERE. The good stuff is THIS WAY.'"

**[0:04 - 0:08] The UV Slideshow**

*Click through 6-8 examples of flowers in UV vs. visible light. Include:*
- Evening primrose (dramatic dark UV center)
- Dandelion (UV bulls-eye pattern)
- Sunflower (UV-absorbing center with UV-reflective petal tips)
- Marsh marigold (striking difference)
- Zinnia (concentric ring pattern in UV)

> "Bees see ultraviolet light — a type of light our eyes simply cannot detect. To us, a field of yellow flowers all looks the same. To a bee, every single flower has a unique pattern, like a fingerprint. They're not just pretty — they're SIGNS. Each pattern tells the bee exactly where the nectar and pollen are."

> "Oh, and here's another mind-bender: bees CANNOT see the color red. A red flower is basically invisible to most bees — it looks black or dark gray. So who pollinates red flowers? Hummingbirds, mostly. Flowers and their pollinators have evolved TOGETHER over millions of years, each shaping the other."

**[0:08 - 0:10] Nurse Nina's Transmission**

> "TRANSMISSION FROM NURSE NINA: Hello, recruits. I'm Nurse Nina. I take care of the young bees and keep the hive running. But I also understand something critical: without flowers, there is no hive. And without us, many flowers cannot reproduce. We NEED each other. Today I'm going to show you just how deep this partnership goes — and why it matters for everything you eat. Pay attention. This one's important."

---

### EXPLORE — "The Pollination Machine" (20 minutes)

**[0:10 - 0:16] How Pollination Works**

> "Let's start with the basics. Pollination is how flowering plants reproduce. But most plants can't do it alone. They need a delivery service."

*Use a large diagram or build a simple model with craft supplies as you talk:*

> "Here's what happens, step by step:"

1. **A flower produces nectar** — a sugary liquid — deep inside itself. This is the bait.
2. **A bee lands on the flower** to drink the nectar. As it crawls around, pollen grains from the flower's anthers (the male part) stick to the bee's branched hairs. Remember those branched hairs from Session 1? THIS is why they matter.
3. **The bee flies to another flower** of the same species. Some of the pollen grains rub off onto the stigma (the female part) of the new flower.
4. **The pollen travels down** to the ovule. A seed begins to form.
5. **The seed grows into a fruit** that protects it. The fruit falls, gets eaten, or gets carried away — spreading the seed to grow a new plant.

> "Without step 2 and 3 — without the BEE — steps 4 and 5 never happen. No pollination, no seeds. No seeds, no new plants. No new plants, no food."

**[0:16 - 0:20] The Food Web Collapse Game**

*Spread the "What Would Disappear?" food cards face-up on a table or the floor. Each card shows a food item.*

> "Let's play a game. I'm going to name a type of pollination. If a food depends on that type, it gets removed from our table. Let's see what's left."

**Round 1:** "Remove everything that depends on BEE pollination."
- Remove: apples, almonds, blueberries, cherries, cucumbers, avocados, watermelon, coffee, pumpkins, strawberries, onions, broccoli

**Round 2:** "Remove everything that depends on ANY insect pollination."
- Remove: chocolate (pollinated by midges), vanilla, mango, passion fruit

**Round 3:** "Remove anything that depends on animal pollination of any kind."
- Remove: bananas (bat-pollinated), agave

*Look at what's left: basically grains (wheat, rice, corn — wind-pollinated), some root vegetables, and meat/dairy (though cattle feed depends partly on pollinated crops).*

> "One out of every three bites of food you eat exists because a pollinator — most often a bee — visited a flower. If bees disappeared tomorrow, we wouldn't starve immediately, but our diet would become extremely limited, and the entire global food system would be in crisis."

**[0:20 - 0:25] Buzz Pollination — The Sonic Secret**

> "Now here's something AMAZING that most people have never heard of. Some plants — tomatoes, blueberries, eggplants, cranberries — have a special type of pollen that's locked inside tube-shaped anthers. The only way to shake it loose is with a very specific vibration."

*Hold up a cardboard tube with pom-poms inside. Shake it gently — nothing comes out. Then vibrate it rapidly — pom-poms fall out.*

> "This is called BUZZ POLLINATION or sonication. Certain bees — especially bumblebees — grab onto the flower and vibrate their flight muscles WITHOUT flapping their wings. They buzz at a specific frequency: approximately middle C on a piano, around 261 hertz."

*Play the audio clip of middle C and then a recording of a bee buzz-pollinating. They should sound very similar.*

> "Honey bees actually CANNOT do this. Only bumblebees and some other wild bees can buzz-pollinate. This is one reason why wild bee diversity matters — different bee species do different jobs."

**[0:25 - 0:30] Symbiosis and Co-Evolution**

> "The relationship between bees and flowers is what scientists call MUTUALISM — both sides benefit. The flower gets pollinated, and the bee gets food. But it goes even deeper. Over millions of years, flowers and their pollinators have literally shaped each other's evolution."

Give quick examples:
- Flowers evolved bright colors, sweet scents, and nectar guides to attract specific pollinators
- Bees evolved longer tongues to reach deep nectar, better color vision to spot flowers, and branched hairs to carry pollen
- Some relationships are incredibly specific: certain orchids can only be pollinated by one species of bee

> "This is called CO-EVOLUTION. It's like a dance that's been going on for over 100 million years. Bees and flowers are ancient partners. And Nurse Nina wants us to understand: when we protect bees, we protect flowers. When we plant flowers, we protect bees. It's a circle."

### MAKE — "Seed Bomb Factory" (25 minutes)

**[0:30 - 0:33] Mission Briefing**

> "Nurse Nina has given us a mission. Bee habitats are shrinking — lawns replace meadows, pesticides kill wildflowers, and development destroys nesting sites. But we can fight back. Today, we're making SEED BOMBS."

> "A seed bomb is a ball of clay, compost, and native wildflower seeds. You toss it onto bare ground — a vacant lot, a neglected corner of a yard, an empty garden bed — and wait for rain. The clay protects the seeds from birds and wind. The compost feeds them. And when they sprout, they create food and habitat for bees and other pollinators."

**[0:33 - 0:36] Regional Seed Discussion**

> "Before we start, let's talk about what seeds we're using. This is IMPORTANT. We're using a mix of NATIVE wildflower seeds — plants that naturally grow in our region. We never want to plant invasive species that could harm the local ecosystem."

*Display or list the specific seeds in your mix. Briefly describe 3-4 of them:*
- What they look like when they bloom
- Which pollinators they attract
- When they bloom (early spring, summer, fall — ideally the mix covers multiple seasons)

**[0:36 - 0:50] Seed Bomb Construction**

*Distribute materials. Each participant makes 3-5 seed bombs.*

**Step-by-Step (display on screen or poster):**

1. Take a handful of clay (about the size of a golf ball)
2. Flatten it into a disc in your palm
3. Add a pinch of compost (about 1 teaspoon) to the center
4. Add a pinch of seed mix (about 1/2 teaspoon) on top of the compost
5. Fold the clay around the filling, sealing the seeds inside
6. Roll into a smooth ball between your palms
7. Set on wax paper to dry (they'll need 24-48 hours to fully harden)

*Circulate and assist. Use this time for casual conversation:*

- "Where would you throw your seed bombs?"
- "What season do you think is best for throwing them?" (Early spring or late fall, depending on region)
- "Why do you think we use clay instead of just scattering seeds on the ground?" (Protection from birds, holds moisture, prevents wind dispersal)

**[0:50 - 0:55] Seed Bomb Labels**

*Give participants index cards to make labels for their seed bombs (to take home):*

Label should include:
- "Honey Beez Seed Bomb"
- Seed types included
- Instructions: "Throw onto bare soil in a sunny spot. Water if no rain within a week. Watch for sprouts in 2-4 weeks."
- "Made by [Name] for the bees"

### PLAY — "Pollination Relay Race" (15 minutes)

**[0:55 - 0:58] Setup and Rules**

> "Nurse Nina has set up a foraging course. You need to pollinate as many flowers as possible before time runs out. Here's the catch: you're the bee."

*Point to the 5 flower stations around the room — each is a plastic cup with Cheetos or cheese puffs (representing pollen-covered anthers).*

**Rules:**
1. Teams line up relay-style
2. First runner "bee" flies (runs) to Flower Station 1
3. They must pick up a Cheeto WITH THEIR ELBOWS (no hands — simulating how bees collect pollen on their bodies, not in their hands)
4. Carry it to Flower Station 2 and deposit it (cross-pollination!)
5. Pick up a new Cheeto from Station 2 and carry it to Station 3
6. Continue through all 5 stations, then run back and tag the next teammate
7. If you drop a Cheeto, you must pick it up with your elbows again (no hands!)
8. Team with the most successful "pollination events" (completed transfers) in 5 minutes wins

*Alternative for less mess: use cotton balls dusted with chalk or cocoa powder. The powder transfer between stations visually shows pollen being moved.*

**[0:58 - 1:07] Race Rounds**

**Round 1 (3 minutes):** Standard relay as described above.

**Round 2 (2 minutes):** "PESTICIDE ROUND" — Randomly close 2 of the 5 flower stations (put an X over them). Now bees must travel further between remaining flowers.

> "This is what happens when wildflower habitat disappears. The bees have to fly further, use more energy, and pollinate fewer flowers. This is a real problem bees face every day."

**Round 3 (2 minutes — optional if time allows):** "BUZZ POLLINATION" — At one station, the Cheetos are inside a closed paper bag. To get one out, the bee must hum/buzz at the bag for 3 full seconds (simulating buzz pollination frequency) before a teammate opens it.

*Tally scores across rounds. The pesticide round should naturally produce lower scores — use this as a teaching moment.*

> "Notice how your scores dropped when we removed flower stations? In the real world, bees are facing exactly this crisis. Habitat loss means fewer flowers, longer flights, less food, and weaker hives. Those seed bombs you just made? They're part of the solution."

---

### CONNECT — "Pollination Puzzle Game + Bridge" (10 minutes)

**[1:07 - 1:12] Pollination Puzzle Game**

*Pull up the Pollination Puzzle Game on the main screen.*

> "Nurse Nina's final test for today. The Pollination Puzzle Game challenges you to match pollinators with the right flowers and build a thriving ecosystem."

Walk through the game:
- Players must connect pollinators to compatible flowers
- Some flowers require specific pollinators (buzz pollination plants need bumblebees)
- The goal is to create a balanced food web where all plants get pollinated
- More diverse pollinator populations = more resilient ecosystem

*Run as group activity or individual if devices are available. Spend 3-5 minutes playing.*

**[1:12 - 1:15] Lifecycle Poster and Bridge**

*Hand out the Lifecycle Poster to each participant.*

> "This poster shows the complete cycle: flower to bee to pollination to seed to new flower to bee again. It's a circle that has been spinning for over 100 million years. Put this up somewhere you'll see it."

*Read the bridge to Session 4:*

> "TRANSMISSION FROM NURSE NINA: Excellent work, recruits. You understand the flower connection now. But here's what I haven't told you yet: when Scout Sunny brings that nectar back to the hive, it's MY job to turn it into honey. And the hive I work in? It's an engineering masterpiece. Next session, Queen Bea herself is going to show you the inside of the hive. You'll learn why hexagons are the most perfect shape in nature, how we turn flower nectar into liquid gold, and why our building techniques are studied by architects and mathematicians around the world. Also, there will be beeswax candles. You're welcome. — Nurse Nina"

## Differentiation Tips

### For Participants Who Need More Support
- Pre-roll clay balls for the seed bomb activity so they only need to add seeds and compost
- Provide a pollination sequence strip (step 1, step 2, step 3...) with pictures they can arrange in order instead of recalling from memory
- During the relay race, allow hand use instead of elbow-only if motor skills are a challenge
- Pair with a partner during the food web collapse game to discuss which cards to remove
- Provide a simplified version of the lifecycle poster with blanks to fill in rather than full labeling

### For Advanced Participants
- Challenge them to calculate: "If one bee visits 100 flowers per trip, and a hive has 30,000 foragers, how many flower visits happen per day if each forager makes 10 trips?" (Answer: 30 million)
- Ask them to research: "What's the economic value of bee pollination in the US per year?" (Approximately $15-20 billion)
- Have them design a "pollinator garden plan" — sketch a garden layout with plants that bloom in spring, summer, and fall to provide year-round food
- Challenge: "Why can't we just pollinate everything by hand?" (Labor cost, scale — it's been tried in parts of China where bees have declined, and it's incredibly slow and expensive)
- Ask them to compare buzz pollination frequencies across different bee species

### For Participants Who Finish Early
- Provide a "Pollinator Matching" card game (match pollinators to the plants they specialize in)
- Offer materials to build a model flower with visible nectar guides using tissue paper and markers
- Let them create additional seed bombs with different seed mixes and label them for different environments (shade, sun, wet, dry)

## Take-Home Extension Activities

### Seed Bomb Deployment
> "Your seed bombs need 24-48 hours to dry fully. Once dry, find a spot that could use some wildflowers — a bare patch in your yard, a corner of a community garden, or an unused strip of land (with permission!). Toss your seed bombs, and keep a journal of what happens over the next few weeks. Photograph any sprouts."

### UV Flower Hunt (with help)
> "Ask a parent or guardian to help you search online for 'flowers under ultraviolet light.' Find 3 flowers you recognize and compare what they look like to humans vs. what they look like to bees. Which one has the most dramatic difference? Sketch both versions."

### Kitchen Pollination Audit
> "Open your refrigerator and kitchen cabinets. How many foods can you find that exist because of pollination? Make a list. Anything with seeds inside a fruit — apples, strawberries, peppers, tomatoes, squash — is a pollination product. How long is your list?"

### Family Dinner Connection
> "At your next family meal, identify at least 3 items on the table that exist because of pollinators. Share one fact you learned today about how pollination works. Challenge: can you find a single meal where NOTHING depends on pollination? (Hint: it's almost impossible.)"

## Session Notes for Facilitator

- **Seed Sourcing:** Source native wildflower seeds from a local nursery or reputable native seed company. Avoid big-box store "wildflower mixes" that may contain non-native or invasive species. If unsure, contact your local agricultural extension office for region-appropriate species.
- **Allergy/Sensitivity Note:** Check for allergies before using Cheetos or cheese puffs in the relay. Substitute with cotton balls and chalk powder if needed. Also, some participants may have sensitivities to the clay or compost — have gloves available.
- **Mess Factor:** This session is messy. The seed bomb activity involves clay and soil; the relay involves cheese dust or powder. Cover tables with butcher paper or plastic tablecloths. Have a cleanup plan and extra paper towels ready.
- **UV Photos:** If you cannot find good UV flower photos, an alternative hook is to use a blacklight flashlight on white paper with "invisible" highlighter drawings — demonstrating that there are things our eyes simply cannot see. Some white flowers also glow dramatically under blacklight.
- **Seed Bomb Drying:** Seed bombs need to go home in bags or containers. Have ziplock bags or small paper bags available. Warn participants not to seal the bags until the bombs are fully dry (24-48 hours) or they'll mold.
- **Timing Note:** The seed bomb activity is very engaging and tends to run long. If time is tight, reduce the number of seed bombs per participant from 5 to 3, or skip the labeling step and send labels home as a take-home activity.
