# Session 4: "Bee Builders"

**Honey Beez Curriculum | Ages 9-12 | 85 minutes**

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this session, participants will be able to:

- Explain why hexagons are the most efficient shape for the honeycomb structure (maximum space, minimum material)
- Describe the 6-step process of honey production from nectar collection to capped comb
- Identify the role of beeswax in hive construction and explain how bees produce it
- Apply hexagonal geometry principles in a hands-on engineering challenge

---

## Facilitator Script

### HOOK — "The Problem That Took Humans 2,000 Years" (10 minutes)

**[0:00 - 0:04] The Ancient Question**

> "I have a math problem for you. It sounds simple. Here it is: What is the best shape for tiling a flat surface so that you use the LEAST amount of material to create the MOST amount of space?"

> "In other words: if you're building a wall of storage containers, and you want to waste zero space between them, AND you want to use the least possible material for the walls — what shape should each container be?"

*Give participants 30 seconds to discuss at their tables.*

> "Squares? Good guess — they tile perfectly with no gaps. But the walls aren't the most efficient. Circles? They're strong, but look what happens when you pack circles together..."

*Draw circles packed together on a whiteboard. Point to the wasted space between them.*

> "See all that wasted space? Terrible for storage. Triangles? They tile perfectly too, but the angles are sharp and the walls-to-area ratio is bad."

**[0:04 - 0:07] The Answer**

> "In 36 BC, a Roman scholar named Marcus Terentius Varro proposed that HEXAGONS were the answer. But he couldn't PROVE it mathematically. Neither could anyone else. For over TWO THOUSAND YEARS, the greatest mathematicians in the world tried and failed to prove that hexagons are the most efficient tiling shape."

> "It wasn't until 1999 — less than 30 years ago — that a mathematician named Thomas Hales finally published the proof. It's called the Honeycomb Conjecture. Hexagons win. They use the least wall material to create the most enclosed area of any shape that tiles a flat surface."

*Pause for effect.*

> "And BEES figured this out millions of years ago. Every honeycomb in every hive on the planet is built from perfect hexagons. Not sort-of hexagons — PERFECT ones, with angles of exactly 120 degrees, walls that are just 0.05 millimeters thick, and a slight 13-degree backward tilt so honey doesn't drip out. Bees are better engineers than we are. And they do it in the dark, standing on each other's backs."

**[0:07 - 0:10] Queen Bea's Transmission**

> "TRANSMISSION FROM QUEEN BEA: Recruits. I am Queen Bea. I've been watching your progress, and I'm... adequately impressed. Today, you enter my domain. Everything in this hive was built by my workers — 60,000 bees working in perfect coordination. The comb you'll see today contains hexagonal cells for raising young, storing pollen, and holding honey. My workers produce the building material from their own bodies. They built this structure in complete darkness, and it is mathematically perfect. Try to keep up. — Queen Bea"

### EXPLORE — "Hive Engineering and Liquid Gold" (20 minutes)

**[0:10 - 0:16] Honeycomb Geometry Deep Dive**

*Display a high-resolution honeycomb photo on screen.*

> "Let's look at the numbers. A single honeycomb cell is about 5.2 millimeters wide — roughly the width of a pencil. The walls are about 0.05 millimeters thick. That's thinner than a sheet of paper. And yet, a frame of honeycomb that weighs about 100 grams — less than a quarter of a pound — can hold over 2 kilograms of honey. That's a 20:1 weight ratio. Imagine if your backpack could hold 20 times its own weight."

*Hand out hexagon geometry reference sheets.*

> "Why are hexagons so good? Three reasons:"

1. **Perfect tiling:** Hexagons fit together with zero wasted space — no gaps.
2. **Minimum perimeter:** Of all shapes that tile perfectly, hexagons have the shortest total wall length for the enclosed area. Less wall = less wax needed.
3. **Structural strength:** The 120-degree angles distribute force evenly in all directions. Push on one wall, and the force spreads out to six neighboring cells. It's like a natural shock absorber.

*Quick demonstration: give each table a sheet of regular paper and a sheet of paper folded into a zigzag/accordion pattern (simulating the hexagonal wall structure). Place a book on each. The flat paper collapses. The accordion paper holds the book.*

> "That zigzag is basically what hexagonal walls do — they distribute weight across the structure."

**[0:16 - 0:24] How Honey Is Made — The 6-Step Process**

> "Now for the alchemy. Honey starts as flower nectar — a watery sugar solution, about 80% water. By the time bees are done with it, honey is less than 18% water and basically never spoils. Archaeologists have found 3,000-year-old honey in Egyptian tombs that was still edible. Here's how they do it:"

*Display or draw the 6-step process:*

**Step 1 — Collection:** A forager bee visits 100-1,500 flowers per trip. She drinks nectar through her proboscis (tongue) into her honey stomach — a special storage chamber separate from her regular stomach. A single forager carries about 40 milligrams of nectar per trip.

**Step 2 — Enzymatic Breakdown:** While the nectar is in the honey stomach, enzymes begin breaking down complex sugars (sucrose) into simple sugars (glucose and fructose). The main enzyme is called invertase. This chemical transformation is already happening during the flight home.

**Step 3 — Mouth-to-Mouth Transfer:** Back at the hive, the forager regurgitates the nectar to a house bee. The house bee chews it for about 30 minutes, adding more enzymes and beginning to reduce the water content. She may pass it to several other house bees — each one adding enzymes and removing water.

**Step 4 — Cell Deposit:** The processed nectar is deposited into a hexagonal cell. At this point, it's still too watery to be honey — roughly 70% water.

**Step 5 — Evaporation:** House bees fan the cells with their wings, creating airflow across the surface of the nectar. Thousands of bees fanning simultaneously — their wings beat at about 230 times per second — reduce the water content from 70% down to less than 18%. This can take 1-3 days.

**Step 6 — Capping:** When the water content drops below 18%, the honey is "ripe." Worker bees seal the cell with a thin cap of fresh beeswax. This cap keeps the honey sterile and preserved. A capped cell of honey can last essentially forever if the cap stays intact.

> "To make ONE POUND of honey, bees collectively fly approximately 55,000 miles — more than twice around the Earth — and visit about 2 MILLION flowers. A single worker bee produces about 1/12th of a teaspoon of honey in her entire lifetime."

**[0:24 - 0:27] Beeswax Chemistry**

> "So where does the building material come from? Beeswax is produced by worker bees from special glands on their abdomens. When a bee is between 12 and 18 days old, these wax glands are most productive. The bee eats honey — about 6-7 pounds of honey to produce 1 pound of wax — and the glands convert the sugar into wax."

> "The wax comes out as tiny, translucent flakes — smaller than a pinhead. The bee chews these flakes with her mandibles, mixing in enzymes to soften them, and then carefully attaches the wax to the comb. A single cell requires about 80-120 of these tiny flakes."

> "Fresh beeswax is nearly white. The yellow and brown colors you see in old comb come from pollen, propolis (tree resin that bees collect), and foot traffic over time."

**[0:27 - 0:30] Honey Tasting (Optional) and Cultural Connection**

*If doing the honey tasting, now is the time. Set out 3-4 honey varieties with tasting spoons.*

> "Notice how each honey tastes different? That's because the flavor depends entirely on which flowers the bees visited. Clover honey is mild and sweet. Buckwheat honey is dark and molasses-like. Orange blossom honey has citrus notes. Every batch of honey is a snapshot of the flowers blooming in that area during that season."

*While participants taste:*

> "Here's our cultural connection for today. In many Celtic and British traditions, there's a custom called 'Telling the Bees.' When something important happened in the household — a birth, a death, a marriage — someone had to go to the beehives and TELL the bees. They would knock on the hive, drape it in cloth for a death or ribbons for a wedding, and formally announce the news."

> "If you didn't tell the bees, people believed they would leave, stop producing honey, or die. This tradition goes back centuries and was practiced well into the 1900s. It reflects a deep, old belief that bees are not just insects — they're members of the family. Partners. Listeners. In many cultures, bees are the bridge between the human world and the natural world."

### MAKE — "Beeswax Candle Rolling + Hexagonal Engineering" (25 minutes)

This section has two activities running simultaneously. Participants complete the candle first (10-12 minutes), then move to the engineering challenge (13-15 minutes).

**[0:30 - 0:32] Candle Introduction**

*Show the completed sample candle.*

> "Queen Bea has given us real beeswax — the same material her workers produce from their bodies — to make candles. Beeswax candles burn cleaner than paraffin candles, produce a natural honey scent, and have been made by humans for thousands of years. Here's how we roll them."

**[0:32 - 0:42] Beeswax Candle Rolling**

*Distribute beeswax sheets and wicking. Display instructions on screen.*

**Step-by-Step:**

1. **Warm the wax:** If the wax is stiff, gently warm it with your hands or the facilitator can pass a hair dryer over it briefly. It should be pliable but not melting.
2. **Place the wick:** Lay the wick along one short edge of the beeswax sheet, leaving about 1 inch of wick extending beyond the top edge. Press the wax edge firmly around the wick.
3. **Begin rolling:** Starting from the wick edge, roll the wax tightly and evenly. Keep the bottom edge aligned so the candle will stand flat.
4. **Maintain pressure:** Roll firmly but gently — too loose and the candle won't burn properly, too tight and the wax may crack.
5. **Seal the edge:** When you reach the end, gently press the final edge into the candle body. If it won't stick, warm it slightly with your fingers.
6. **Trim the wick:** Trim the top wick to about 1/4 inch above the wax.

*Circulate and help. Common issues:*
- Wax cracking: needs more warming
- Uneven rolling: realign and reroll from the beginning
- Wick falling out: press the first fold more firmly around the wick

> "Fun fact: the beeswax you're holding was produced by real bees. They had to eat approximately 6 pounds of honey and fly thousands of miles to make enough wax for your candle. Every beeswax product is concentrated bee labor."

*Set completed candles aside.*

**[0:42 - 0:45] Engineering Challenge Introduction**

> "Guard Gary has a challenge for you. You've learned that hexagons are the strongest, most efficient tiling shape. Now PROVE IT. Your team must build a structure using toothpicks and marshmallows that can hold the most weight."

*Distribute materials: each team gets 50 toothpicks and 30 marshmallows.*

**Challenge Rules:**
1. Build a structure that is at least 4 inches tall
2. The structure must have a flat top surface where a paper cup can be placed
3. You will load the cup with pennies/washers to test weight capacity
4. You have 10 minutes to build
5. The team whose structure holds the most weight before collapsing wins
6. HINT: Think about what you learned about hexagons today. (But teams are free to use any geometry they want.)

**[0:45 - 0:55] Build and Test Phase**

*Start the 10-minute build timer. Circulate and observe without giving direct advice.*

*Common observations to share AFTER building:*
- Teams that use triangles and hexagons tend to outperform those using squares
- Wider bases are more stable than narrow ones
- Connecting nodes (marshmallows) at consistent angles creates more predictable force distribution
- Bracing and cross-supports dramatically increase strength

**Testing (2-3 minutes):**

*When time is up, test each structure:*
1. Place a paper cup on top
2. Add pennies one at a time (or washers for faster testing)
3. Count until the structure fails
4. Record each team's number

> "Let's look at the results. Which shapes did the winning team use? Did anyone try hexagonal geometry?"

*Show your pre-built hexagonal reference structure. Test it alongside the winner if applicable.*

> "In engineering, this principle — that hexagonal and triangular geometries create the strongest structures with the least material — is used in everything from airplane wings to bridge design to satellite panels. Bees knew it first."

### PLAY — "Hive Architecture Engineering Challenge" (15 minutes)

**[0:55 - 0:58] Challenge Setup**

> "Guard Gary's final test. This is the HIVE ARCHITECTURE CHALLENGE. Each team is now a colony of builder bees. Your mission: design and present a blueprint for the perfect bee hive."

*Distribute large poster paper, markers, rulers, and protractors.*

**Requirements for the hive blueprint:**
1. Must include hexagonal cell structure (use the protractors and rulers to draw accurate hexagons)
2. Must designate zones: brood area (baby bees), pollen storage, honey storage, queen's chamber
3. Must show the entrance and how Guard Gary would defend it
4. Must include at least one clever engineering feature inspired by real bee hives (choices listed below)
5. Must have a team name for your colony

**Real hive features to choose from:**
- Propolis coating (anti-bacterial resin layer on all interior surfaces)
- Ventilation system (entrance design and fanning bee positions for temperature control)
- Thermal regulation (the brood area is maintained at exactly 95F / 35C)
- Wax moth defense (structural features to prevent pest intrusion)
- Honey ripening zone (cells positioned for optimal airflow during evaporation)
- Emergency queen cells (special oversized cells where a new queen can be raised if needed)

**[0:58 - 1:07] Design Phase**

*Teams work on their blueprints. Circulate and prompt:*

- "Where did you put the brood area? In a real hive, it's always in the center — the warmest spot."
- "How is Guard Gary going to check every bee that enters? Real hives have a single, narrow entrance."
- "Did you think about temperature control? If the hive gets too hot, bees fan their wings. If it gets too cold, they cluster together and vibrate their muscles to generate heat."
- "Where's the honey stored relative to the brood? In a real hive, honey is always ABOVE the brood area — heat rises, which helps keep the honey at the right consistency."

**[1:07 - 1:10] Presentations**

*Each team presents their hive design (60 seconds max). The group votes on:*
- Most scientifically accurate
- Most creative
- Best engineering feature

*Award titles (not just one winner — spread the recognition):*
- "Queen Bea's Choice for Accuracy"
- "Scout Sunny's Choice for Creativity"
- "Guard Gary's Choice for Defense Design"

## Differentiation Tips

### For Participants Who Need More Support
- Pre-score beeswax sheets with a light fold line to make rolling easier
- For the engineering challenge, provide a photo of a simple toothpick-marshmallow hexagon they can replicate, then build upon
- Offer pre-drawn hexagonal grid paper for the hive blueprint instead of requiring freehand drawing
- During the honey production explanation, provide a simplified 3-step version: (1) Bees collect nectar, (2) Bees process and dry the nectar, (3) Bees seal the honey in wax cells
- Pair with a supportive partner for the candle rolling — one holds, one rolls

### For Advanced Participants
- Challenge them to calculate: "If a hexagonal cell is 5.2mm wide and a frame is 48cm x 23cm, approximately how many cells fit on one side of a frame?" (Hint: it's roughly 4,000-5,000 per side)
- Ask them to research: "Why exactly 120-degree angles? What happens to force distribution at other angles?"
- For the engineering challenge, impose an additional constraint: "Your structure must also be able to withstand a lateral push (earthquake test)"
- Have them write a mathematical comparison: surface area to volume ratio of hexagonal cells vs. circular vs. square cells of the same area
- Challenge: "Design a hive for a different environment — underwater, in space, or in the desert. What would you change?"

### For Participants Who Finish Early
- Provide additional beeswax sheets to make a second, more creative candle (tapered, multi-colored, etc.)
- Offer a "Hive Math" puzzle sheet with geometry problems based on hexagonal structures
- Let them create a step-by-step illustrated guide to honey production that could teach a younger child

## Take-Home Extension Activities

### Beeswax Candle Burn Observation
> "Light your beeswax candle at home (with adult supervision). Notice the smell — real beeswax has a faint honey scent. Watch how it burns compared to a regular candle. Beeswax burns slower and cleaner. Time how long your candle lasts and report back."

### Hexagon Hunt
> "Hexagons are everywhere in nature and engineering. This week, find and photograph at least 5 hexagons in the real world. Ideas: honeycomb (obviously), turtle shells, basalt columns (like the Giant's Causeway), snowflakes, bolts and nuts, soccer balls, floor tiles. Bring your photos to share."

### Tell the Bees
> "The Celtic tradition of 'Telling the Bees' is about respecting the connection between humans and nature. Write a short letter to the bees — what would you tell them? What would you thank them for? What would you promise to do for them? This can be a poem, a letter, or a drawing."

### Engineering Extension
> "Using materials you find at home (straws, toothpicks, cardboard, tape), try to build the tallest free-standing structure you can that uses hexagonal geometry. Measure its height and test how much weight it can hold. Can you beat your team's score from today?"

### Family Honey Tasting
> "If your family has different types of honey at home (or can visit a farmers' market), do a honey tasting together. Compare colors, scents, and flavors. Can you taste the difference between honeys from different flowers? Which is your family's favorite?"

## Session Notes for Facilitator

- **Beeswax Temperature:** Beeswax sheets crack when cold and become too soft when hot. Room temperature of 70-75F is ideal. If your space is cold, warm sheets with a hair dryer on LOW for 5-10 seconds before distributing. Do NOT microwave beeswax sheets.
- **Allergy Check:** Before the honey tasting, check for honey allergies (rare but possible) and any sensitivity to bee products. Children under 1 should never eat honey (botulism risk), but this shouldn't apply to the 9-12 age range.
- **Marshmallow Alternative:** If marshmallows are not suitable (dietary restrictions, allergies), use gumdrops, clay balls, or play-doh as connectors for the engineering challenge.
- **Time Management:** This session has the most content of all four sessions. If time is tight, the two flexible sections are: (1) the honey tasting can be shortened or done as participants work on candles, and (2) the Hive Architecture Challenge presentations can be reduced to 30 seconds each.
- **Candle Safety Note:** Emphasize to participants that the candle should only be lit at home with adult supervision. Include a small card with the candle that says: "Light with adult supervision only. Never leave a burning candle unattended."
- **Cleanup:** Beeswax can leave residue on tables. Wax paper or butcher paper under the work area makes cleanup much easier. Marshmallow residue on hands can be washed with warm water.
- **Emotional Tone:** This final session should feel celebratory. Queen Bea's final transmission is meant to be a "graduation" moment. Read it with gravitas and let participants feel the accomplishment of completing the four-session arc. If you have time, let participants share their favorite moment from the entire program.
